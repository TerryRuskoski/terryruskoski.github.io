#Teaching Statement - Terry Ruskoski
<img src="/assets/images/Headshot.png" 
     alt="Terry Ruskoski" 
     style="float: right; width: 150px; border-radius: 8px; margin-left: 1rem; margin-bottom: 1rem;">

My approach to teaching is rooted in curiosity, mentorship, and the belief that all students are capable of developing scientific thinking when given support, encouragement, and opportunities to connect concepts to the world around them. As a first-generation college student who began my own journey to a Ph.D. in Biochemistry at College of DuPage, I understand firsthand the challenges many students face while trying to navigate higher education, discover where they belong, and build a future in science. My own journey was not linear, and it was shaped by mentors and educators who helped me recognize opportunities, develop confidence, and persist through uncertainty. I hope to provide that same support for my students.

After earning a bachelor's degree in sociology, I realized that my interests and talents lay in the sciences. Returning to College of DuPage marked the beginning of a transition that ultimately led to graduate school and a Ph.D. in Biochemistry from Penn State University. Along the way, I made many of the mistakes common to first-generation students who are learning to navigate unfamiliar systems without the benefit of family experience or professional networks. Those experiences have shaped my understanding of how important mentorship, transparency, and encouragement can be for student success.

I earned my Ph.D. in Biochemistry from Penn State University, where my research focused on the structure and mechanism of metalloenzymes and radical enzymes. Throughout graduate school, I mentored undergraduate and graduate students, coordinated complex collaborative projects, and served as President of the Biochemistry and Molecular Biology Graduate Student Association. I also pursued formal training in pedagogy through graduate coursework and teaching programs, including coursework in educational theory and practice prior to and during my student teaching experience, as well as certification in online teaching. These experiences reinforced my belief that effective teaching involves much more than delivering information; it requires creating environments in which students feel supported, challenged, and capable of success.

In the classroom, I strive to cultivate curiosity rather than simply deliver information. Biology, biochemistry, and microbiology are filled with stories that reveal the remarkable interconnectedness of life. Whether discussing metabolism, microbial ecology, animal physiology, or evolution, I aim to help students understand not only what we know, but how scientists ask questions and uncover answers. I want students to recognize science as an active process of discovery rather than a collection of facts to memorize.

I recognize that learning science is an iterative process. Students are often encountering concepts for the first time, and it is unrealistic to expect complete mastery after a single exposure. I therefore strive to scaffold learning by helping students identify the essential ideas and relationships while building complexity over time. Through repetition and revisiting concepts in new contexts, students develop increasingly sophisticated understanding and confidence. My goal is not simply for students to memorize information for an exam, but to help them construct frameworks that will allow them to continue learning throughout their careers and lives. In this way, I view science education as a process of continual growth rather than a sequence of isolated topics.

I believe that students learn best when they are encouraged to make connections across scales and disciplines. From molecules to ecosystems, biological systems are dynamic, interdependent, and constantly changing. Helping students appreciate these relationships allows them to develop a deeper understanding of the material and equips them with habits of mind that extend far beyond the classroom.

Because of my own experiences, I am particularly interested in supporting first-generation and nontraditional students. Many students are balancing work, family responsibilities, and uncertainty about their futures while simultaneously learning how to navigate higher education itself. I believe educators can serve as stewards who create systems for success by making expectations transparent, fostering a sense of belonging, and helping students develop the confidence to pursue opportunities they may not yet see for themselves.

Ultimately, my goal is not simply to teach biology, biochemistry, or microbiology. I hope to inspire students to see the beauty, complexity, and wonder of the living world and to help them develop the confidence and critical thinking skills necessary to pursue their own paths, whatever those paths may be. If students leave my courses with a deeper appreciation for science, greater confidence in their abilities, and the understanding that they belong in the scientific community, I will consider that a success.
