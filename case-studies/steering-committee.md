# Redesigning a First-Year Graduate Curriculum Around the Learner Experience

## Overview

As a graduate student representative on Penn State’s departmental Steering Committee, I helped evaluate and redesign the first-year graduate curriculum after recurring student concerns revealed a disconnect between the program’s intended learning goals, the experience of students moving through the curriculum, and the outcomes the program was actually producing.

The challenge was not that individual courses lacked thoughtful design or defensible goals. Many had been created by faculty who remained deeply invested in their original purpose. But over time, the curriculum as implemented had become fragmented. Expectations and instructional approaches varied across courses and instructors, student experience lacked coherence, and important program-level learning goals were not being achieved consistently.

By bringing the cumulative student experience into the discussion while helping separate the quality of the original course designs from the performance of the curriculum as a whole, I helped reframe a contentious conversation around implementation, alignment, and program outcomes rather than individual faculty decisions. The existing three-course sequence was ultimately retired and replaced with a redesigned first-year curriculum.

## Impact at a Glance

* Represented the graduate-student perspective in departmental curriculum decision-making
* Identified gaps between intended learning outcomes, the cumulative learner experience, and the skills students were ultimately expected to demonstrate
* Helped reframe a contentious faculty discussion from individual course quality to system-level implementation
* Contributed to the redesign and replacement of all three courses in the first-year graduate curriculum
* Helped strengthen alignment between early coursework and skills students would later be expected to demonstrate

## Program Scope

The first-year curriculum served students entering a biochemistry and molecular biology graduate program from varied academic backgrounds, including biology, chemistry, microbiology, molecular biology, and biochemistry.

One central goal was to establish a shared foundation across this diverse incoming cohort while preparing students for the analytical and communication skills required later in graduate training.

The curriculum involved multiple courses and faculty instructors, each responsible for different content areas and instructional modules. This distributed model created an additional design challenge: individual components could function as intended while producing an inconsistent or overwhelming experience when encountered as a complete program.

## The Challenge

Student concerns about the first-year curriculum were recurring rather than isolated.

One particularly challenging core course was taught in modules by several faculty members. Each instructor approached the course’s purpose differently. Some emphasized foundational undergraduate material, others focused on primary literature, and others emphasized current research topics.

Viewed independently, each approach had merit.

Viewed from the perspective of a first-year student moving through the entire course, however, the experience lacked coherence. Expectations shifted between modules, which ran concurrently, workloads accumulated, and students were often unsure what skills they were actually expected to develop.

The broader curriculum also did not consistently reinforce some of the analytical skills students would later need for major program milestones, particularly reading, interpreting, and critically evaluating scientific literature in writing and verbally. Recent changes to the qualifying examination also shifted the analytical skills students were expected to demonstrate, leaving portions of the existing curriculum poorly aligned with current program milestones.

The problem was therefore larger than any individual instructor or course:

**The components had been designed individually, but the learner experienced them as a system.**

## My Approach

As the graduate representative on the Steering Committee, I brought both recurring student feedback and my own experience moving through the curriculum into faculty discussions.

Rather than evaluating each course or module independently, I encouraged the committee to examine the curriculum from the learner’s perspective:

* What was each component intended to accomplish?
* What did students actually experience when those components were combined?
* Were expectations consistent across instructors and courses?
* Were students repeatedly practicing the skills the program ultimately expected them to demonstrate?
* Had implementation changed over time in ways that moved the curriculum away from its original purpose?

This distinction became particularly important when faculty members who had helped design or teach the existing curriculum understandably reacted defensively to criticism of it.

I did not believe the original design itself was necessarily the problem. Instead, I helped reframe the conversation around how the curriculum had evolved in practice and whether its current implementation still produced the intended outcomes.

That shifted the discussion away from **“Was this course designed badly?”** and toward **“Is the system, as students experience it now, accomplishing what we want it to accomplish?”**

## Process Improvements Implemented

### Evaluated the curriculum as an integrated learner experience

Instead of treating student concerns as isolated complaints about individual instructors or courses, the committee examined how expectations, workload, instructional styles, and skill development accumulated across the first-year program.

### Re-centered discussion on shared outcomes

Faculty perspectives differed on what particular courses should accomplish. Focusing on the capabilities students needed to develop across the program created a common reference point for evaluating curriculum design.

### Distinguished design problems from implementation drift

Recognizing that a well-conceived program can change as instructors, content, expectations, and institutional needs evolve helped depersonalize the redesign process and made substantive discussion easier.

### Strengthened development of core analytical and communication skills

The redesigned curriculum distributed activities such as reading, interpreting, critiquing, writing about, and presenting scientific literature more intentionally across the program rather than relying on isolated exposure.

### Incorporated the learner perspective into institutional decision-making

Student experience became evidence for evaluating how the curriculum functioned as a whole, complementing faculty expertise about individual content areas and educational goals.

## Outcome

The Steering Committee developed and approved a broader redesign of the department’s first-year graduate curriculum.

All three existing courses were retired and replaced, with the revised curriculum implemented for subsequent incoming students.

The redesign created an opportunity to align coursework more intentionally across the first year and to embed important analytical and presentation skills throughout the curriculum rather than expecting students to integrate a collection of independently designed experiences on their own.

## Long-Term Impact

This experience changed how I think about educational and organizational systems.

A system can contain individually reasonable components and still produce a poor overall experience. Evaluating each piece independently can therefore miss the actual source of the problem.

It also reinforced the importance of implementation drift. Programs evolve as people, priorities, practices, and institutional expectations change. Periodically returning to the original purpose—and comparing it with what users actually experience and what the system is ultimately expected to produce—is essential to maintaining an effective program.

## Reflection

This project reinforced a principle that now shapes much of how I approach systems improvement:

**People experience systems as wholes, even when organizations manage them as separate parts.**

The faculty involved had deep expertise and many of the individual curriculum components had sound rationales. The breakdown became visible only when we examined how those components interacted over time, what students experienced while navigating them, and whether the curriculum as a whole was still producing the outcomes the program intended.

Effective redesign therefore required more than identifying what was not working. It required understanding the system’s original intent, separating design from implementation, making space for different stakeholder perspectives, and creating enough shared understanding for change to become possible.
